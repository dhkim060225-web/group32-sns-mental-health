Hook

Does scrolling more mean feeling worse?

Almost everyone believes it: the longer students spend on social media, the worse they feel and the worse they do in class. We took that conventional wisdom to a dataset of 705 students from 110 countries to see how far it holds.

In this data, every extra hour a day goes with a score about 0.70 points lower. The rest of this story is about whether to believe what that number seems to say.

Start the story

How a question about grades became a question about wellbeing

We started in Semester 1 with a familiar claim: heavy social media use lowers academic performance. Arguing it out on Slack, we kept finding holes.

Scrolling short videos is not the same as messaging a project group. Stressed students might reach for their phones more, so the phone may be a symptom rather than a cause. Step by step, the question narrowed, changed outcome, and picked up the variables that might be doing the real work.

  1. 1

    Hypothesis

    Passive entertainment use goes with a lower GPA than active or study use, and the overall link weakens once stress is controlled. H₀: μpassive = μactive.

  2. 2

    Data strategy

    The only data we could get has no GPA and no passive-versus-active measure. We switched the outcome to the self-rated mental health score and planned to control for academic level. H₀: β₁ = 0, H₁: β₁ < 0.

  3. 3

    From testing to prediction

    Simple linear regression: slope, R², a 95% confidence interval, and a train/test split to check the model on students it has not seen.

  4. 4

    Data collection

    Secondary data (Option B). We audited it: one duplicated student, 110 countries, scores that are identical within whole groups.

  5. 5

    Causal check

    Double machine learning, a sleep pathway test, and three system maps that compare what the statistics show with what theory suggests.

The four questions that changed our direction

AssumptionsCould stress make students use social media more, instead of the other way round?

We stopped treating hours as the only possible cause.

ClarificationShould all social media use count the same?

We separated passive scrolling from active and study use. In this dataset, the most-used platform is our rough stand-in.

EvidenceDoes the link survive once sleep and stress are taken into account?

We built controls into every model and tested sleep as a pathway.

ViewpointsCould social media used for group projects actually help?

We compared platforms instead of assuming every hour is equal.

The data, and what it can't tell us

We used the Students' Social Media Addiction dataset from Kaggle: 705 rows and 13 self-reported variables. Its page does not say how students were recruited, so we cannot call it a random sample of any population.

Two rows (Student IDs 242 and 274) are identical in every answer, so we kept one. That leaves n = 704 students from 110 countries. Half of those countries contribute a single student.

Our hypotheses

H₀: β₁ = 0. Daily hours are not linearly related to the mental health score.

H₁: β₁ ≠ 0 (two-sided). Our earlier, directional version was β₁ < 0.

Why α = 0.05

A false alarm (Type I) would tell counsellors that hours harm wellbeing when they don't, leading to blanket screen-time rules and wasted sessions. A miss (Type II) would leave struggling heavy users unflagged. Both costs are real but neither is life-threatening, so we used the conventional 0.05. The calculator in the Evidence chapter shows where that choice matters.

What the data shows, and how sure we can be

Scroll through the chart. Each step adds one layer to the same 704 dots.

A cloud with a clear tilt

Students who report more hours tend to report lower mental health. The correlation is r = −0.80, unusually strong for survey data.

The regression line

Predicted score = 9.69 − 0.70 × hours. The 95% confidence interval for the slope is −0.74 to −0.66 (t = −35.5, p < 0.001), and hours alone explain 64% of the variation.

At 6 hours a day the model predicts 5.5; at the average of 4.9 hours, 6.2. Nobody reported less than 1.5 hours, so the intercept of 9.69 is an extrapolation.

Where the model is most wrong

A good way to test a model is to find the cases it gets badly wrong. The six biggest misses are all students in Pakistan and Bangladesh who use social media a moderate 3.8 to 4.1 hours and sleep 7.5 to 8.1 hours.

The model expects about 7. They report 5. Hours don't explain them; where they live might.

Countries move together

Each circle is a country with at least 20 students. Swiss, Danish and Japanese students use less and score higher. American, Mexican and Indian students use more and score lower.

Country alone explains 69% of the variation in scores (63% after adjusting for its 110 categories), more than hours do.

Within a country, the slope is flatter

Comparing students inside the same country, the slope drops from −0.70 to −0.58, about 18% weaker. Part of the original pattern was a difference between countries, not between students.

Because students from one country resemble each other, they aren't independent. Allowing for that, the slope's 95% interval widens to −0.80 to −0.61, about 2.4 times wider than the textbook interval.

Does the type of platform change the relationship?

This was the heart of our original idea. Among platforms with at least 20 users, the slope runs from −0.41 (WhatsApp) to −1.05 (Twitter). A test of whether one common slope fits every platform rejects it: F(8, 650) = 3.59, p < 0.001.

R² varies even more, from 0.23 to 0.77, but R² is a poor way to compare platforms. It depends on how spread out usage is: WhatsApp users all use 5 to 8 hours (SD 0.59), which squeezes R² down. Slopes are the fairer comparison.

Does the model work on students it has never seen?

Level 5 asks a different question from Level 4: not "is the link real?" but "do predictions hold on new data?" We trained on a random 80% of students and tested on the other 20%, then repeated that with 200 different random splits.

Test R² averages 0.64 (range 0.48 to 0.73), the same as the in-sample 0.64, so the model is not overfitting. Its typical error (RMSE) is 0.66 points, 40% smaller than guessing the average for everyone (1.10).

Choose your significance level

α is a policy, not a fact: the same p-value can mean "act" for one group and "wait" for another. Move the slider and watch which conclusions survive.

    With 704 students the headline result survives any α you choose. α only matters for the small-sample questions, which is exactly where a false alarm could push a platform-specific policy that doesn't deserve it.

    How much of the hours effect survives other explanations?

    We re-estimated the slope while accounting for one explanation at a time. The top rows use ordinary regression. The pink rows use double machine learning (DML): random forests first remove everything the background variables can predict about both hours and mental health, using 5-fold cross-fitting. The slope is then estimated from what is left.

    With background removed by DML, the effect is −0.63 (95% CI −0.68 to −0.59): smaller than the raw −0.70 but clearly still there. Split by group, it barely moves:

    A warning about "controlling for everything". Adding the addiction score and conflict count wipes the effect out (−0.06 in regression, +0.06 with DML). That does not show hours don't matter. Both variables are most likely consequences of heavy use, and addiction correlates −0.95 with the outcome. Controlling for them removes the very pathway we are trying to measure.

    The sleep pathway

    Heavier users sleep less: each extra hour of social media goes with 0.71 hours less sleep. Holding hours fixed, each extra hour of sleep goes with a 0.19-point higher score.

    Multiply the two and about 20% of the hours association runs through sleep (indirect effect −0.14, 95% bootstrap CI −0.18 to −0.10). That is a lever a counsellor can actually work with.

    Mediation from observational data assumes nothing unmeasured, such as stress, drives both sleep and mood. That assumption is doubtful here.

    And grades, our original question?

    The dataset has no GPA, but it asks whether social media hurts each student's academic performance. Students who say yes use 5.5 hours a day against 3.8 for those who say no (Welch t = 25.4, p < 0.001).

    The pattern is almost too tidy. Nobody under 3 hours says yes, and everybody over 6 hours does. Real survey answers rarely switch from 0% to 100% like that, which is one reason we treat this dataset with caution.

    The conventional wisdom points the right way but tells the wrong story

    Hours matter: about 0.6 points per hour even after removing background differences. But hours are tangled up with country, sleep and platform, nothing here shows which way the arrow points, and the dataset is too clean to be trusted completely.

    Why variables that aren't significant, or aren't measured, still matter

    Not significant is not the same as no effect. The slope did not differ significantly by academic level (p = 0.81) or gender (p = 0.31). But only 27 students are in high school, too few to detect anything but a large difference.

    Stress and purpose of use were never measured. Both appear in every serious account of student wellbeing. If stress drives both scrolling and low mood, every number on this page overstates what cutting screen time could achieve.

    If we ignored country, we would overstate the hours effect by about a fifth and claim more certainty than the data allows.

    If we ignored sleep, we would miss a pathway worth about 20% of the effect, and a practical place to intervene.

    If we ignored platform, a blanket rule would treat a WhatsApp group chat the same as an endless TikTok feed, even though their slopes differ.

    How much should you trust this dataset?

    • Self-reported scales rarely correlate this strongly. Addiction score and mental health correlate at −0.95.
    • All 12 users of LINE, KakaoTalk and VKontakte report exactly the same score as each other, as do all 25 Indian WhatsApp users. In 13% of countries with two or more students, every student has the same score.
    • Academic-impact answers jump from 0% to 100% across usage bands.
    • One student appears twice. Residuals are not normal (Shapiro–Wilk p < 0.001), and the score only takes six whole-number values.

    Our verdict: this works well as a teaching dataset for practising the full chain from population to reasoning. It is not strong enough to set real policy for real students.

    What we would tell a university

    Counsellors

    Treat heavy use as a reason to start a conversation, not a diagnosis. Ask about sleep first: it carries a fifth of the link and is easier to change.

    Students

    Notice what your hours are for. The same time on a group chat and on an endless feed may not affect you in the same way.

    Researchers

    Measure stress and purpose of use, follow the same students over time, and sample within countries before making claims about everyone.

    What we learned about thinking with data

    The course's six layers became a checklist. At each one we found something that changed how strongly we could state our conclusion.

    1. Population

      We wanted "students who use social media". We don't know how these 705 were chosen, so our claims stop at this dataset.

    2. Sampling

      Secondary data with a large n, but clustered by country and not random. This is the "is big good?" trap: size does not cure bias.

    3. Sampling distribution

      With n = 704 the central limit theorem makes the slope's sampling distribution close to normal, but only if students are independent. Country clustering breaks that.

    4. Inference

      The confidence interval and the test agree, as they always must: −0.74 to −0.66 excludes zero, and p < 0.001. Our α came from weighing false alarms against misses.

    5. Prediction

      Test-set R² of 0.64 across 200 splits. Good on averages, roughly ±1.3 points for any one student.

    6. Reasoning

      Causal maps and double ML moved us from correlation toward causation but stopped at what was measured. Stress and reverse causation remain open.

    If we did it again

    We would run our own survey (at least 50 students per group) asking what the hours were spent on and how stressed students feel. We would follow the same students over a semester to see whether heavy use comes before low mood or after it. And we would look for a natural experiment, such as a phone-free campus policy, that could serve as an instrument for a causal estimate.
